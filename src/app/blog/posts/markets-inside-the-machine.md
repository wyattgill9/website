# Markets Inside the Machine: How China's Economy Actually Works (November 2026)

_Prices and profits decide most of what China makes and buys. But the Communist Party controls the levers that decide who gets to make it: people, credit, land, licences and quotas. This is a guide to how the system really works._

---

China's government is currently fighting a campaign against too much competition.

Solar-panel makers, battery producers and electric-vehicle companies have competed so hard that Beijing has rewritten its competition rules, drafted a ban on below-cost selling, asked industry associations to work out what an industry's "average cost" should be, and fast-tracked dozens of merger reviews. The official name for the disease is "involution": competition that wears everyone out and rewards nobody.

A command economy could not have this problem, and China doesn't have one. Its trouble is not a planner setting quotas for factories. Its markets work almost too well, inside a structure that keeps feeding them. Many of those factories exist because local governments competed for them with cheap land, subsidised energy and equity stakes from local government funds, and because state-steered credit flowed toward favoured industries. When the glut drove prices down, the fix was not left to the market either. It came as a Party-backed campaign run through regulators and industry associations.

That episode is the whole system in miniature. Ownership and everyday market activity in China are spread across the state, private firms, households and foreign investors. Prices, profits and competition allocate most goods and much of the investment. But the power to set priorities, pick the people who run the important institutions and override decisions belongs to the Chinese Communist Party. The Party uses that power through ministries, state shareholders, state-controlled banks and local governments. It works mainly by steering five things: **personnel, credit, land, licences and fiscal quotas.** It seldom needs to tell a factory what to produce.

What follows takes each lever in turn: who pulls it, how it works, and where it is under strain in November 2026. The strains are serious. The collapse of land revenue has gutted local budgets. Investment is shrinking, and the factories it built are selling abroad what households at home won't buy. A local-debt clean-up is racing a hard deadline. Industrial policy now runs mostly through finance. And Beijing and the IMF disagree about how large China's public debt really is.

## The control room

Start at the top. The Politburo and its Standing Committee set the economic line. The full Central Committee approves long-range direction at its plenums: in October 2025 its Fourth Plenum adopted the recommendations that became the 15th Five-Year Plan, which the National People's Congress (NPC) approved in March 2026.

The rest of the year follows a fixed calendar. The Politburo reviews the economy at meetings conventionally held in April, July and December. Each December the Central Economic Work Conference sets the stance for the following year. In March the State Council's Government Work Report and the NPC budget turn that stance into numbers. For 2026 those were a growth target of 4.5–5%, an official deficit of about 4% of GDP and RMB 1.3 trillion of ultra-long special treasury bonds.

So far the headline is on track. GDP grew 4.6% in the first nine months of 2026 and 4.5% in the third quarter, helped by an export boom driven by demand for AI hardware. Momentum is fading, though. The official manufacturing PMI, a survey of factory activity, fell to 49.3 in October, below the 50 line that separates expansion from contraction. The IMF has trimmed its 2026 forecast to 4.5%, citing higher energy prices from the Middle East conflict and weak domestic spending, and expects 4% in 2027.

The real deliberation happens in a few central Party commissions that most outsiders have never heard of. The Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission, led by Xi Jinping with Li Qiang as deputy, sets top-level economic strategy, such as a unified national market, the anti-involution drive and supply-chain security. The Central Financial Commission oversees financial stability and coordinates the central bank and regulators. The Central Financial Work Commission handles Party discipline and personnel across the financial system. Other commissions steer technological self-reliance and approve cross-cutting reforms. By public accounts, one official, He Lifeng, runs the offices of both the economic and the financial commissions and is secretary of the financial work commission. The membership and workings of these bodies are only partly disclosed.

The commissions mostly don't run agencies directly. They issue decisions and documents, often as joint notices from the general offices of the Party Central Committee and the State Council. These bind ministries and local Party committees, and they often come before legislation or take its place. On paper, the constitution gives the State Council and the NPC authority over the economy. In practice the Party decides first and the state carries it out.

The State Council's agencies act as translators. The National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC) writes the plans, approves major projects, sets regulated prices for power, gas and oil products, and maintains the "negative list" of sectors where market entry is restricted. The Ministry of Finance runs budgets, transfers and local bond quotas, and is a direct shareholder in the big banks. The People's Bank of China (PBOC), which is not legally independent, runs monetary policy and manages the exchange rate. Each ministry contains a Party group, usually led by the minister acting as Party secretary, which formally decides major matters and appointments. Ministries and regulators write the rules, and provinces and state firms carry them out.

## Personnel is policy

The lever that holds the system together is the power to appoint.

The Party's Central Organization Department manages every post at vice-ministerial rank and above. That covers provincial Party secretaries and governors, ministers, and the leaders of the most important central state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and state financial institutions. Lower posts are filled by ministry Party groups, by the Party committee of SASAC (the agency that acts as shareholder for central SOEs), and by provincial and city organization departments. This list of jobs the Party fills, the _nomenklatura_, is the real organisation chart of the Chinese economy. Company law says shareholders and boards choose the chair of a bank or an oil major. In practice the organization department chooses and the board ratifies.

Executives move around this chart, rotating between regulators, state banks, SOEs and provincial posts. A banker whose next job may be a provincial governorship has every reason to treat the Party's priorities as their own.

The way officials are judged shapes what they do. In the growth-at-all-costs 2000s, GDP counted above almost everything else, so building things helped careers. Cadre evaluations still count growth and fiscal results, but under Xi Jinping harder constraints weigh more heavily: containing debt risk, meeting environmental targets, avoiding safety disasters, keeping social stability and showing political loyalty. Binding targets such as farmland protection, energy and carbon intensity, and safety go straight into evaluations. Indicative targets, including the growth target, guide behaviour without binding it.

There is also a stick. The Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and the National Supervisory Commission share staff and post inspection teams inside ministries, central SOEs and financial institutions. They can detain state functionaries, including managers of SOEs and state banks, under a procedure called _liuzhi_ that sits outside ordinary criminal procedure.

Taken together, this gives the Party a way to override nearly any decision that matters. It can act through Party groups in ministries and Party committees in SOEs and state banks. It can send central inspection tours (_xunshi_) to check whether localities and firms are following central policy. It can issue joint Party–State Council documents that outrank departmental rules, or a leader can write instructions (_pishi_) on an individual case. And it can launch campaigns with deadlines, which often move faster than statute. The anti-involution drive is one such campaign, and so is the order for local financing vehicles to leave the government-borrowing business by June 2027.

## The local machine

Beijing sets the direction. Local governments carry it out, and they do most of the spending.

China has five tiers of government: the centre, 31 provincial-level units, prefecture-level cities, counties and townships. At every tier a Party secretary outranks the head of government. Officials describe authority as running along two axes. _Tiao_ are vertical lines down from the ministries, and _kuai_ are horizontal blocks under the local Party committee and government. Only a few lines are truly vertical: tax, customs, central-bank branches, the financial regulators' local offices, audit and, in part, statistics. Most other bureaus, from development and reform to land and market regulation, are under "dual leadership", and in practice the local government dominates.

The asymmetry is deliberate. Local leaders hold most of the economic levers. The centre keeps the tools it needs to police them: tax collection, finance, statistics and audit.

Money is at the heart of the relationship, and the split is lopsided. The tax-sharing rules favour Beijing on most of the big taxes. Domestic VAT, the largest tax, is divided 50/50. Corporate and personal income taxes are split 60/40 in the centre's favour. Consumption tax and taxes collected at customs go entirely to the centre, while localities keep smaller taxes such as deed and land-related levies. Localities still keep more in total, RMB 12.21 trillion of general-budget revenue in 2025 against the centre's RMB 9.40 trillion, but spending is far more lopsided: local governments account for most general-budget spending, so they depend on central transfers and on bond quotas that Beijing hands out. And for years they depended on land.

### The land machine, and its collapse

In China all urban land belongs to the state, and rural land belongs to village collectives. That gave local governments an unusual business model. They acquire collective land, convert it to urban use and sell fixed-term use rights, usually by auction. Industrial land is often priced below cost to attract factories. The rest of the sales brought in money, and land revenue became a pillar of local finance. Together with borrowing through local companies, it paid for a building boom no other country has matched. By the end of 2025 China had more than 50,000 km of high-speed rail and 199,000 km of expressways, and 58 mainland cities run about 13,000 km of urban rail, more than the rest of the world combined.

That pillar has cracked. Land-sale revenue was RMB 8.7 trillion in 2021. By 2025 it was RMB 4.15 trillion, less than half the peak, after a fourth straight year of double-digit decline. In 2026 the fall has sped up. Revenue was down 25.2% year on year in January–February, and secondary reports of finance-ministry data put the first-half decline at 31.5%, to RMB 977.8 billion.

As private demand has fallen away, the buyers that remain are mostly state-owned. According to CRIC data reported by Caixin, 14 major state-owned developers increased their land spending by 20% in 2025 and accounted for half of the total value in CRIC's tally. Local governments' own investment vehicles, which once propped up land auctions, have had to scale back too.

Beijing also limits what localities can do with land. The Ministry of Natural Resources sets annual quotas for construction land and enforces a farmland "red line" with satellite monitoring and inspections. Local governments cannot trade those constraints away.

### Places competing like countries

Chinese localities compete for investment much as countries do, offering tax rebates, cheap land, subsidised energy, equity stakes through local guidance funds and a friendly regulator. Hefei is the classic case: in 2020 its city-backed investors rescued the struggling carmaker NIO in return for a stake and its headquarters. Beijing picks the race and the localities run it, which is why China has about 130 electric and hybrid car brands rather than one planned champion. The trouble is that many of them chase the same favoured industries, such as EVs, solar, batteries and steel, and end up building duplicate capacity.

The centre's response is the "national unified market" agenda. It uses fair-competition reviews and bans on discriminatory local subsidies and market barriers, and in August 2025 the NDRC promised to standardise investment promotion with lists of encouraged and prohibited practices. The tax rebates that localities used to offer are now curbed. Enforcement depends on central inspections and the market regulator's reviews, and there is little evidence on how far local protectionism has actually declined.

## The debt hangover

For years local governments got around limits on their own borrowing by borrowing through companies they owned: local government financing vehicles, or LGFVs. These companies raised money for local projects, and lenders treated them as backed by the government. Cleaning up the resulting "hidden debt" is now the main constraint on local behaviour.

Beijing is dismantling the system on a deadline. By the end of June 2027 every LGFV must do three things. It must clear its hidden debt. It must give up its government-financing role. And it must either clear its operating financial debt or get consent from creditors holding at least two-thirds of it. A three-year, RMB 6 trillion programme for 2024–2026 swaps hidden debt for official refinancing bonds. Its final RMB 2 trillion tranche falls in 2026, alongside RMB 800 billion a year of new special bonds earmarked for debt resolution.

On paper it is working. Finance Minister Lan Fo'an has put hidden debt at RMB 14.3 trillion at the end of 2023 and RMB 10.5 trillion a year later, according to Caixin. An official report on 3 November 2026 said the number of remaining platforms was down 92% from March 2023 by the end of September, and that their operating financial debt was down 79% by the end of June.

Some exits are cosmetic. Chinascope, citing unnamed experts, says some platforms "completed only procedural exits through renaming, mergers, or debt swaps, without developing independent market-based businesses." The exit statistics may overstate how much has really changed.

The clean-up has a cost: lower investment. Bloomberg reported that RMB 1.38 trillion of 2025's RMB 4.4 trillion special-bond quota, about 31%, went to repaying debt, and infrastructure investment fell 2.2% that year. In 2026 the squeeze has spread. Fixed-asset investment fell 8.1% year on year in January–October, with infrastructure down 5.1%, private investment down 11.4% and property investment down 21.6%. Beijing is taking on a bigger share of new government debt: 56% in 2025, up from 48% the year before, by Yicai's reading of the government work report.

Consider what this means for a city that wants to build something in 2026. The project must be on an approved list. Its special-bond money comes from a national quota that the finance ministry allocates through the province, and the project must show it will earn enough to cover the debt. The city may instead have to use the bond money to swap hidden debt or pay overdue bills. Its LGFV can no longer borrow on its behalf. What remains is ultra-long treasury-bond money for national priority projects, policy-bank loans and concessions to SOEs. If the city carries a "red" debt-risk rating, it may be barred from starting new projects at all.

### How big is the hole?

The answer depends on which books you open, and China keeps four. A **general public budget** covers taxes and most current spending. A **government fund budget** holds land sales, special-bond proceeds and, according to CSIS, all of this year's ultra-long treasury bonds. A small **state-capital budget** collects SOE profits, and a **social-insurance budget** handles pension and medical funds.

The headline deficit of about 4% of GDP covers only the first book. Even there, Christine Wong of the East Asian Institute finds a gap of about 5.4% of GDP between spending and revenue, bridged with money carried over from earlier years and transfers from the other books. The IMF consolidates more and gets bigger numbers: a "general budgetary" deficit of 7.4% of GDP in 2025 and 8.7% in 2026. Once LGFVs and government funds are included, its "augmented" deficit is 14.3% and 14.2%.

The debt picture works the same way. Local governments borrow officially through two kinds of bonds. General bonds pay for projects that earn no revenue and count in the deficit. Special-purpose bonds pay for projects meant to earn revenue, are repaid from the fund budget and stay out of the headline deficit. This year's quotas are RMB 4.4 trillion of special bonds and RMB 800 billion of general bonds, plus the RMB 2 trillion of refinancing bonds. The IMF puts the official debt ratio at 68.4% of GDP in 2025, rising to 75.1% in 2026.

Outside the official books there is much more. Caixin reports another RMB 14.8 trillion of LGFV "operating" debt that is not classed as hidden government debt but still leaves local governments responsible for repayment. The IMF puts off-budget debt, including LGFVs and government guidance funds, at about RMB 93 trillion in 2025. That gives an augmented public debt of 126.6% of GDP in 2025, heading for 135.3% in 2026.

Chinese authorities reject this. According to the IMF they "continued to hold an opposing view", arguing that LGFV operating debt should not count. This is a real dispute over definitions, not a settled fact. Depending on where you draw the line between the state and the firms it owns, China's public debt is somewhere between three-quarters of GDP and well over its entire GDP.

## Companies with two bosses

China's state-owned enterprises are real businesses with balance sheets, profit targets and stock-market listings. They are also instruments of policy, and most of what is distinctive about them comes from that double role.

Their scale is enormous. Central SOEs, the roughly 96–98 groups that SASAC oversees on Beijing's behalf, held more than RMB 95 trillion of assets at the end of 2025 and RMB 99.3 trillion by the end of August 2026. In 2025 they earned RMB 2.5 trillion in profit, paid RMB 2.5 trillion in taxes and fees, invested RMB 5.1 trillion in fixed assets and spent RMB 1.1 trillion on R&D. Counting every SOE under the state-asset system, assets totalled RMB 387 trillion at the end of the last five-year-plan period. In the sectors that power everything else, they dominate. In September 2025 SASAC said central SOEs account for "approximately 80 percent of domestic crude oil production, 70 percent of natural gas output and 60 percent of power supply."

The double role is built into governance. Every SOE's articles of association must write in the leadership role of its Party committee. Before the board or management decides on major operations, senior appointments, big projects or large uses of funds, the Party committee must discuss them first, a step called "pre-discussion" (_qianzhi yanjiu_). Academic studies treat it as standard practice in central and local SOEs alike. The Party secretary normally chairs the board, and the general manager is usually deputy Party secretary. Central SOEs have boards with a majority of external directors, but SASAC selects and manages those directors, and many are retired SOE executives. Pay is capped: performance-linked pay can be at most twice base salary, and tenure incentives at most 30% of total pay.

Follow one major investment at a central SOE. Management drafts the project and the Party committee pre-discusses it. The board, with its external-director majority, then decides. SASAC checks it against limits on core business and leverage, and against its scorecard of "one profit, five ratios": total profit plus return on equity, operating cash flow, R&D intensity, labour productivity and the debt-to-asset ratio. If the project is large enough or in a restricted sector, the NDRC must approve it. State banks and bond markets supply the money, and counter-cyclical projects can draw on special treasury-bond funding. Afterwards, discipline inspectors may come to check compliance.

The scorecard is changing. The headline goal for 2026 is for central SOEs' value-added to grow in line with GDP while every key operating indicator improves. Market-value management now counts in appraisals of listed SOEs. And in 2024, for the first time, more than 40% of central SOE investment went to "strategic emerging industries".

Then there is the policy job. SOEs are expected to "expand effective investment" when the economy weakens; SASAC planned about RMB 2 trillion of 2026 investment around what it calls the "six networks". They carry out national projects, buy land when private demand dries up and lead strategic industries. In return they enjoy what economists call a soft budget constraint: cheaper credit, implicit guarantees and, when things go wrong, restructurings arranged by the state. Rhodium Group found that 18 of the 20 largest additions to corporate debt in 2025 were made by state-owned firms.

Does Party oversight make SOEs better or worse run? The evidence is mixed. Chinese quasi-experimental studies find that pre-discussion improves SOE performance. Western studies often find that political goals reduce efficiency.

## Private firms: most of the market, fewer of the guarantees

Most market activity in China is in private hands. By Beijing's own count, private firms produce about 60% of GDP and 80% of urban jobs. Their position, though, rests on discretionary state leverage more than on enforceable rights.

On paper, their protections look substantial. The Private Economy Promotion Law took effect on 20 May 2025. It promises fair competition under a single national negative list for market access, so that anything not prohibited is permitted. It gives private firms equal eligibility for state support (Article 12), access to financing support and to major national research facilities, and protection for their property and for entrepreneurs personally. It also lays down personal sanctions for officials who unlawfully harm private businesses (Articles 71–73). In return it asks private firms for "standardized operation".

Enforcement is the weak point. Jamie Horsley of Yale calls the law a case of "good intentions meet weak government accountability". Firms seldom sue the government, because they need to stay on good terms with the officials who regulate them.

The state also reaches inside private firms. A Party organisation is required wherever a company has three or more Party members, and such organisations are nearly universal in large private firms. In most firms they handle political education, personnel vetting and liaison. In big platforms, finance and strategic technology they can shape compliance and some appointments, though firm-level evidence is thin. Outside the firm, the Party's United Front Work Department guides the All-China Federation of Industry and Commerce, which co-opts entrepreneurs, partly with seats in the national legislature and its advisory body, and relays their complaints.

Sometimes the state is a shareholder. Under so-called "special management shares", state entities take stakes of about 1% in the licence-holding units of internet platforms, with board seats or vetoes attached. Reuters reported in 2021 that such a stake in Beijing ByteDance Technology came with a board seat at the unit holding the licences for Douyin and Toutiao, and that the state had a similar stake in Weibo's main domestic unit. Weibo's US filings say the state investor appoints one of three directors and can veto some content and financing decisions. In 2023 Reuters reported a 1% stake in an Alibaba unit held by an entity under a CAC-backed internet investment fund. More broadly, central and local guidance funds and state venture-capital funds are now major limited partners and direct investors in tech start-ups.

Then there is regulation. The market regulator handled 11,465 unfair-competition cases in the first half of 2026. Separately, it fined seven e-commerce platforms a total of RMB 3.597 billion for food-safety failures, chiefly letting "ghost" delivery operations proliferate. The internet regulator writes the rules on data, cybersecurity and algorithms. Licences, payment and fintech rules, informal "window guidance" and summonses to regulatory "interviews" (_yuetan_) add to the pressure. The discipline system can detain entrepreneurs who are drawn into cases against officials. Day to day, firms depend on local governments for land, permits and subsidies. The new law limits some of this formally, but the discretionary tools remain, and used together they can quickly reprice entire sectors, as platforms, private tutoring and fintech found in 2020–21.

A typical enforcement episode runs like this. A central priority, such as anti-involution in food delivery or data security, sets it off. The market or internet regulator opens an investigation or calls managers in for an interview. Remedies include fines, rectification pledges, licence actions and changes to algorithms. Expectations are relayed through the state's director at the licence-holding unit, the firm's own Party committee and the local government. Legal appeals are rare. The firm adjusts its prices, merchant fees or investment plans to fit the policy.

### The cost of capital

Money tells the same story. Bank loans to private enterprises were RMB 76.07 trillion at the end of the first quarter of 2025, up 7.41%, according to the banking regulator. Set against the central bank's total of RMB 179.53 trillion in loans to companies and institutions, that suggests private firms get up to about 42% of corporate lending. That is the top of the range, because it combines two agencies' series with different coverage. The central bank's narrower measure, loans to privately controlled enterprises, was about RMB 45 trillion at the end of March, or roughly 25%. Nicholas Lardy's PIIE series ends in 2016, when SOEs took 83% of new corporate loans.

Price is easier to measure than volume. Rhodium Group finds that central SOEs' median borrowing rate was 0.40 percentage points below private firms' in 2025, up from 0.10 points in 2015. So far, the new law's promise of equal treatment has not erased the advantage.

Beijing is trying to narrow it. In January 2026 the PBOC set up a RMB 1 trillion relending quota for loans to private firms, of which about RMB 800 billion was outstanding by the end of July. It also created a bond risk-sharing instrument for tech and private firms, backed by RMB 200 billion. But state issuers still dominate the bond market, and the securities regulator still controls the pace of IPOs despite formally "registration-based" rules. Companies listing abroad, including through variable interest entity (VIE) structures, must file with the securities regulator and pass security reviews.

## The plumbing

Every lever described so far relies on one piece of machinery: a banking system the state owns and steers.

Follow the money. Households put their savings into bank deposits. Those deposits go mostly to state-controlled banks. The banks lend to SOEs, LGFVs and, more and more, to priority manufacturing and technology, and they buy government bonds. Whoever directs the banks largely directs the country's savings.

The state directs the banks. The six large commercial banks are majority-owned by the state through Central Huijin and the finance ministry: at ICBC, Huijin held 34.79% and the ministry 31.14% in March 2024, and Huijin's stake reached 35.00% by the end of that year. When they need capital, the state supplies it through special treasury bonds, RMB 500 billion in 2025 and RMB 300 billion in 2026. City commercial banks are mostly controlled by local governments and local SOEs. The three policy banks are wholly state-owned and funded by quasi-sovereign bonds. The chairs and presidents of the big banks are Party appointees, and every state financial institution has a Party committee that pre-discusses major matters, just as SOEs do.

The central bank's toolkit starts with familiar instruments. The policy rate is the 7-day reverse repo rate, at 1.40% since May 2025. Each month a panel of banks quotes the loan prime rate (LPR), in line with policy signals. It has held at 3.00% for one-year loans and 3.50% for five-year loans from May 2025 through October 2026, 17 straight months at record lows. Deposit rates are coordinated through a "self-discipline mechanism" among banks. Formally these are market rates. In practice they are guided.

What stands out is that the central bank allocates money as well as pricing it. Through "structural relending" it lends cheaply to banks for specific purposes. One quota, RMB 1.2 trillion at 1.25%, is for technology innovation and equipment upgrading. Others cover agriculture and small business, private firms, and pledged supplementary lending to the policy banks. Window guidance and sectoral credit guidance push in the same direction: loans to tech SMEs reached RMB 3.63 trillion at the end of 2025, up 19.8%. In property, a "whitelist" steers project loans to approved housing developments.

Picture one big state bank weighing two loans. For a property developer, a city-level coordination mechanism of housing authorities and the banking regulator nominates eligible projects. The bank's credit committee assesses them within the regulator's guidance, and its Party committee pre-discusses large exposures. For a tech firm, the bank can refinance the loan at the central bank's subsidised relending rate, count it toward its tech-lending targets and lean on risk-sharing guarantees. Price reflects policy priority as much as credit risk. Rhodium reports that 58% of new loans in December 2025 were priced at or below the one-year LPR, a sign of heavy discounting for preferred borrowers, most of them state-owned.

The same logic runs through the markets. State banks hold most government bonds, with liquidity support from the central bank, and ultra-long special treasury bonds, with maturities of 20 to 50 years, fund central priorities. When equities wobble, Central Huijin and other state vehicles, known as the "national team", buy index ETFs and bank shares. Capital controls remain. The yuan is managed through a daily central-parity fixing, adjusted by a discretionary "counter-cyclical factor", with trading allowed within 2% either side.

Banks rarely fail outright. Deposits are insured up to RMB 500,000 per depositor, but weak small banks are mostly resolved by merging them, with rural banks folded into larger provincial institutions. The IMF has urged better resolution frameworks and faster recognition of losses.

## The state as venture capitalist

Industrial policy in China now works mainly through finance: funds, relending quotas, bond issues and procurement preferences.

The framework is the 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030), approved on 12 March 2026. Its priorities begin with technological self-reliance, with R&D spending growing more than 7% a year. Next comes a "modern industrial system" built on strategic emerging industries (new energy, new materials, aerospace, the "low-altitude economy") and future industries (quantum, biomanufacturing, hydrogen, fusion, embodied AI, 6G). The plan also stresses domestic demand and household consumption, promising a "markedly" higher consumption share of GDP without giving a number, and more than a third of its major targets relate to livelihoods. For security, a separate agriculture plan published by the State Council in June targets grain capacity of 725 million tonnes and 85% seed self-sufficiency by 2030. For the green transition, the main plan aims to cut carbon dioxide per unit of GDP by 17%. It sets no numerical growth target for the five years, only growth "within an appropriate range".

The plan travels downward. Party recommendations become an NPC-approved outline, which separates binding indicators from "anticipatory" ones. The outline becomes an NDRC annual plan and ministry sectoral plans, then provincial plans and city and county targets. At the bottom it turns into projects, bond quotas, fund investments, credit guidance and procurement lists.

The instruments have grown large. The **National Venture Capital Guidance Fund**, launched by the NDRC and the finance ministry on 26 December 2025, has RMB 100 billion of central capital and a target of RMB 1 trillion, and at least 70% of sub-fund capital must go to seed and early-stage firms. **Big Fund III**, for semiconductors, has RMB 344 billion of registered capital and has jointly set up a RMB 60 billion AI fund. A State Council guideline from January 2025 confines government investment funds to areas where "the market is unable to fully exercise its function". Around the funds sit R&D tax super-deductions, preferential tax rates for high-tech firms, equipment-upgrade subsidies, procurement rules that favour domestic IT in government and SOE purchasing (_xinchuang_), standard-setting, and cheap land and energy in industrial parks. The official slogans are "new quality productive forces" and a "new-type whole-nation system" that mobilises state labs, SOEs, private champions and finance around chokepoint technologies.

Here is how a strategic chip project comes together. National priorities and the five-year plan's list of "chokepoints" pick the target, and the industry ministry and the NDRC endorse it. Big Fund III, whose shareholders include the finance ministry, state firms and local funds, takes a minority stake alongside a provincial guidance fund. State banks lend at rates near or below the LPR, funded by technology relending at 1.25%. The local government provides land and subsidised power in an industrial park, plus tax breaks. Domestic-IT procurement and SOE customers supply the first orders. Later, the securities regulator fast-tracks a listing on the STAR Market. Each state investor's Party committee pre-reviews its participation, and discipline inspectors can later audit any losses.

Each piece of that chain is a lever from earlier in this article: credit, land, licences, personnel and fiscal money, pointed in one direction.

## When everyone builds the same thing

This is where the involution story at the start of this article comes from. Industrial policy relies on credit and on local governments. Local governments compete to host the favoured industries. Cheap credit and cheap land make it rational to build even when rivals are building too. The result is overcapacity, then price wars, and then a campaign to stop them.

What the home market can't absorb goes abroad. Exports rose 36.3% year on year in September and 35.2% in October, and the trade surplus for January–October reached about $1.04 trillion, closing on 2025's full-year record of $1.2 trillion. The Belt and Road has shifted the same way. It used to mean state-bank loans for railways and ports. Now it increasingly means Chinese factories, mines and solar farms abroad, more and more of them built by private firms.

Anti-involution is a coordinated campaign on prices and capacity rather than a single law. Its legal core is a revised Anti-Unfair Competition Law, in force since 15 October 2025, and a pending amendment to the Pricing Law, drafted by the NDRC and the market regulator, that would ban selling below cost to drive out rivals, including through algorithms. Rules issued in October 2025 let industry associations assess average industry costs under government guidance, and in July 2026 the market regulator said it was accelerating work on a "1+1+N" legal system against below-cost dumping, with the Pricing Law amendment still in progress.

The main targets are solar, batteries and EVs, where the regulator fast-tracked 52 merger reviews in the first half of 2026. Compared with earlier campaigns there are fewer hard capacity-cut quotas, and enforcement relies heavily on industry self-discipline and output curbs.

That approach involves a quiet trade-off. When associations publish cost benchmarks under official guidance, some of the power to set prices moves from firms to associations and the government. The IMF is sceptical. In February 2026 its Executive Board said the strategy "should be clarified further and be accompanied by stronger incentives to reduce overinvestment by local governments", and its staff said it "lacks a clearly communicated primary objective". The worry, in other words, is that the campaign goes after prices while leaving in place the local incentives that drive overinvestment.

### What the market prices, and what it doesn't

Anti-involution is a reminder that "market prices" in China come in degrees. Most manufactured goods, consumer goods and many services are priced by the market, within pricing law and, now, anti-involution guidance. Energy is mixed. A growing share of electricity is traded on markets, but grid, household and farm tariffs are regulated. Refined-oil prices follow an NDRC formula that tracks world crude between a ceiling and a floor, and gas has city-gate guidance prices. Local governments set utility prices after hearings. The state sets minimum purchase prices for wheat and rice, and Sinograin, the grain reserve, buys and auctions to keep them steady. National volume-based procurement sets prices for drugs and medical consumables. Land, interest rates and the exchange rate are formally priced by markets but managed, as earlier sections showed. Strategic reserves of grain, pork, oil and metals buy and sell to smooth prices.

## The people who pay for it

For households, the system works like this: savings are high, consumption is low, and the safety net depends on where you are registered.

Gross national saving was 42.1% of GDP in 2025, according to the IMF. Household consumption is only about 40% of GDP, against roughly two-thirds in the United States; final consumption, which also counts government consumption, was 56.6% in 2024 by NBS-based estimates. Per-capita consumer spending was RMB 29,476 in 2025, up 4.4%, but in 2026 spending has stalled: retail sales grew just 0.4% year on year in October. Housing is the dominant household asset, followed by bank deposits and wealth-management products. Home prices have been falling since 2021, which has shrunk household wealth. Household debt stood at 59.4% of GDP in 2025.

Three institutions shape how households live and save. The first is _hukou_, the household-registration system. It links access to local public services, including school places, some health and social-insurance benefits and housing eligibility, to the place where a person is registered rather than where they live and work. Migrant workers therefore get thinner coverage and save more as a precaution. Most cities have loosened their settlement rules, but the megacities still ration residency through points systems.

The second is the safety net. Pensions and medical insurance each run on two tracks, one for urban employees and one for other urban and rural residents, and resident pensions pay very little: by one estimate the average rural pension was about $35 a month in 2024. Most of the funds are pooled locally, and they rely on growing fiscal subsidies.

The third is land. Rural households hold contract rights to collective farmland, which can be leased or transferred and in pilot areas mortgaged, and use rights to homestead plots, which generally cannot be sold outside the collective. Ownership stays with the collective, and rural land reaches the market only through government-controlled conversion or limited pilot markets. So rural families' land is hard to sell or borrow against, and turning it into urban land has to go through the local government.

Beijing now says it wants more consumption, and the 15th Five-Year Plan stresses it. It has preferred targeted measures to broad stimulus, and some money goes directly to households. In 2026 the state is paying RMB 250 billion in consumer trade-in subsidies (down from RMB 300 billion in 2025) and RMB 200 billion for equipment upgrades. A nationwide childcare subsidy pays RMB 3,600 a year, tax-free, for each child under three. Central funding was about RMB 90 billion in 2025, and more than 30 million babies had received payments by February 2026. Larger sums go elsewhere. RMB 800 billion of this year's ultra-long bonds is for "major national strategies and security capacity-building". Education gets 3.1% of GDP in the 2026 general budget and health 1.5%, while general-budget spending as a whole has fallen to 20.4% of GDP from 25.0% in 2015, by Wong's analysis. On the IMF's broader measure, general-government revenue was 25.2% of GDP in 2025 and spending 33.0%.

### Labour without bargaining

Workers have few institutional ways to push their share up. The All-China Federation of Trade Unions is the only legal union federation, and it is led by the Party. Independent unions are banned. Enterprise unions are often run by managers or HR staff and funded by a mandatory 2% payroll fee paid by employers. Collective contracts are widespread on paper but rarely involve real bargaining, so wages are set mainly by the labour market and by provincial minimum wages. The rules say those must be adjusted "at least once every two years", though a 2015 notice relaxed that in practice to every two to three years.

Individual disputes go through mediation, then compulsory arbitration, then the courts. Collective protests are handled as threats to stability. The Labour Contract Law requires written contracts, limits repeated fixed-term renewals and mandates severance pay, but it is enforced less well for migrant and informal workers. Most gig workers are not classified as employees. They are covered by guidelines on "new forms of employment", occupational-injury insurance pilots for delivery and ride-hailing workers, and guidance on algorithmic fairness.

## The outsiders

Foreign firms work under rules that have opened up on paper even as security powers have widened.

The opening is real. The Foreign Investment Law promises national treatment outside a "negative list" that no longer restricts manufacturing. The remaining limits are mostly in telecoms, media, some agriculture and a few services. Free-trade zones have shorter lists, and the Hainan Free Trade Port goes furthest. Profits can be repatriated once taxes and audits are done, through banks that check the paperwork under foreign-exchange rules. Portfolio investors get in through Stock Connect, Bond Connect, direct access to the interbank bond market and the QFII/RQFII schemes. Party organisations exist in many joint ventures, especially those with SOE partners, and in some wholly foreign-owned firms, where they usually have little influence.

The security overlay is real too. Under the personal-information, data-security and cybersecurity laws, cross-border data transfers require CAC security assessments, standard contracts or certification. The thresholds have been relaxed, but the treatment of "important data" is still discretionary. Beijing has also built a full set of countermeasures. They include the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law, the Export Control Law and its dual-use list, national-security reviews of investment, and exclusion from procurement under "secure and controllable" rules. They also include the Unreliable Entity List, which reportedly added US and EU defence and critical-mineral firms in 2026; that report comes from trade-monitoring sources and should be checked against MOFCOM's announcements. A revised Anti-Espionage Law has chilled due-diligence and consulting work.

Rare earths show how these tools are used. Licensing for seven medium and heavy rare earths, in force since April 2025, still applies. A further package that MOFCOM announced in October 2025, including an extraterritorial "0.1% content" rule, was suspended until 10 November 2026. The day before it lapsed, MOFCOM extended the suspension to 10 January 2027, matching the US–China trade truce, which was extended by two months around Xi's visit to Washington in late September. The export surge keeps that truce fragile.

## What we know, and what we don't

Put the pieces together and the Chinese economy is easier to read. The Party sets direction and chooses the people. Ministries turn direction into quotas, prices and rules. Local governments compete with land, subsidies and their own companies. State banks channel household savings toward whatever the plan favours. SOEs carry counter-cyclical investment and buy land when private demand falls short. Private firms account for most market activity, inside a perimeter of licences, investigations and state stakes that can move quickly. Most of the time the levers work quietly through markets. When they don't, the Party overrides with campaigns, inspections and appointments. In 2026 the result is an economy hitting its growth target with a lopsided mix: strong supply, weak demand and a surplus the rest of the world has to absorb.

Parts of that picture call for caution. Formal rules and practice diverge: company law empowers boards that Party committees pre-empt, and "market" interest rates are coordinated. Some numbers are contested. Whether China's public debt is 68.4% of GDP or 126.6% depends on definitions Beijing disputes. LGFV exit statistics may overstate real change. Rhodium Group's estimate that real growth in 2025 was 2.5–3.0%, against the official 5.0%, is itself a contested methodological claim. Some evidence is thin. What is known about Party committees' influence inside private firms comes mainly from anecdote and varies by sector, and the central commissions' workings are only partly public. A few figures here, namely the first-half 2026 land-revenue fall, the 2026 additions to the Unreliable Entity List and the private share of bank lending, rest on secondary reporting or analyst calculation and should be checked against primary releases.

Two dates are worth watching. The trade truce and the rare-earth suspension both run out on 10 January 2027, and that will test how Beijing uses its new economic-security tools. LGFVs must leave the government-financing business by June 2027, and that will show whether the debt clean-up has changed how local governments behave or only what their companies are called. A real shift toward households would show up elsewhere: in a numeric consumption target, much bigger rural pensions, or hukou reform that comes with money for public services. None has happened yet.

This article has mostly avoided labels, because each one fits only in part. Beijing calls the system a "socialist market economy"; most specialists say "state capitalism" or "party-state capitalism". It has the structures of state corporatism, with one licensed body each for workers and for business, but not the bargaining between them that corporatism implies. Comparisons with 1930s Germany fit one feature, controlling private firms without owning them, and fail on the rest: China's state owns far more, markets set most prices, and the economy is wide open to trade. What can be described with some confidence is how it works. Markets are vigorous enough to push prices below cost. Above them sits a Party-state that answers with what it controls, which is not output quotas but personnel, credit, land, licences and money.
